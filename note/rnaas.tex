\title{The Abiotic Methane Ceiling Sets a Floor on Detectable Biospheres}

% Add your ORCID as \author[0000-...]{...} if you have one.
\author{Siddhant Sharma}
\affiliation{Blue Marble Space Institute of Science, Seattle, WA 98154, USA}
\email{siddhant.sharma@bmsis.org}

\begin{abstract}
Flux based biosignature assessment compares an inferred surface methane flux against
a ceiling on abiotic production. The ceiling in current use is a deliberately
conservative upper bound, adopted as a single number. It lies more than an order of
magnitude above one published maximum for the same process and an order of
magnitude above another, above
anything its own generating equation yields at modern Earth crustal production
rates, and a factor of three below Earth's own biological methane flux. The choice
of ceiling therefore fixes the smallest biosphere an observation can reveal, and the
span of defensible choices is wider than the spectroscopic uncertainty of the
retrieval it is compared against. That dependence can be reported at no additional
computational cost.
\end{abstract}

\section{A bound doing the work of a likelihood} \label{sec:setup}

\citet{wogan2026} infer surface gas fluxes for an Archean-like TRAPPIST-1\,e by
inverting a coupled photochemical and climate model, then ask whether the inferred
fluxes are consistent with life. The step from flux to biosignature runs through a
set definition: methane fluxes above $3.7\times10^{10}\ \flux$ are taken to exceed
what ocean bottom serpentinization can supply. That value is the 99.7th percentile
of the distribution of maximum abiotic fluxes computed by \citet{kt2018}, adopted,
as \citet{wogan2026} say, for simplicity.

As a set boundary the choice is sound and conservative by design. Its consequences
appear at the following step, where the complement of the biosignature region is
taken as the abiotic alternative and the resulting odds ratio is used as a proxy for
the Bayes factor between life and no life. A bound chosen to be generous then does
the work of a likelihood, and its position stops being a matter of caution and
becomes a quantitative input.

\section{Locating the ceiling} \label{sec:locating}

Three comparisons place it.

The first does not depend on reading any number as a realised flux, because it sets
the ceiling against other published maxima. \citet{guzman2013} modelled methane
production by serpentinization from the kinetics of the reactions involved, with
carbon dioxide as the limiting reactant, and report a maximum surface flux of
$6.8\times10^{8}\ \flux$ for an Earth mass planet, 1.74 dex below the ceiling.
\citet{merdith2020} give an upper case in which the methane flux approaches the
hydrogen flux, 1.12 dex below it. Even the Archean maximum of \citet{fiebig2007},
which describes a more vigorous Earth than the one being modelled, falls 0.60 dex
short. The adopted ceiling is the highest published maximum for this process by
more than an order of magnitude.

The second uses the generating equation itself. The lower corner of the four
parameter ranges sampled by \citet{kt2018} is built entirely from Earth calibrated
values: their modern crustal production rate, 10 wt\% FeO in newly produced crust,
the 0.25\% conversion of FeO to hydrogen that they calibrate against observation,
and the hydrogen to methane ratio of 12 measured in ultramafic hosted vent fluids.
Their Equation 6 then returns 0.012 Tmol yr$^{-1}$, or $\log \Fch = 7.67$, a little
below the directly measured mid-ocean ridge values. Their distribution runs from
what Earth does to the most generous case they were willing to entertain, and the
adopted percentile sits 2.90 dex above its lower end.

The third asks what that equation reaches without leaving Earth-like conditions.
Holding crustal production at the modern Earth value and setting the other three
factors to the generous end of their published ranges, Equation 6 gives
$\log \Fch = 9.87$. The adopted ceiling is 0.70 dex above that, so it is reachable
only by invoking crustal production above modern Earth's. The factor of ten allowed
in their range is an early Earth provision, and \citet{kt2018} note that whether
such rates are likely for terrestrial exoplanets is an open question. Applied to a
planet of roughly Earth size, that provision is carrying much of the ceiling.

Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} sets all of this against published estimates of Earth's
realised abiotic methane production \citep{schindler2000, kasting2005, emmanuel2007,
fiebig2007, fiebig2009, cannat2010, keir2010, catling2017, klein2019, portella2019,
merdith2020}, compiled from the supplementary material of \citet{thompson2022} and
from \citet{merdith2020}. Every estimate for modern Earth lies below the ceiling,
the largest, from continental hydrothermal emissions, by 1.5 dex. Six seafloor
of the nine seafloor estimates, spanning mid-ocean ridges, off-axis vent fields,
submarine volcanism and subduction related sites, cluster between
$\log \Fch = 7.3$ and 8.1; the remaining three lie between 8.4 and 8.8.

\section{What the ceiling costs} \label{sec:cost}

A ceiling implies a minimum detectable biosphere. \citet{wogan2026} adopt
$1.122\times10^{11}\ \flux$ for modern Earth's biological methane flux, following
\citet{catling2017}. The ceiling lies a factor of three below that. A planet whose
biosphere produced methane at less than a third of Earth's rate could not be called
inhabited on this criterion at any signal to noise. Placing the ceiling at the Earth
calibrated value instead moves that floor to roughly one part in 2400 of Earth's
biological flux.

The span is also large next to the measurement it is compared against. The retrieval
returns a 68\% credible interval 1.5 dex wide for $\log \Fch$. Table A1 of
\citet{wogan2026} gives that width under every combination of gas abundances fixed
to their true values, and the narrowest entry is 0.1 dex, the limit of what perfect
abundance knowledge would buy on this target. Two of the three framings above, 1.74
and 2.90 dex, exceed the interval actually achieved, and the third, 0.70 dex, is
about half of it; all three are several times the 0.1 dex floor. A
posterior width and a range of candidate thresholds are different objects, but both
enter the same decision in the same units, and here the geochemical term is the
larger.

\section{The dependence can be reported} \label{sec:reportable}

None of this is concealed in the original work. \citet{wogan2026} state that they
adopt a single value for simplicity, note that the inhabited and lifeless flux
regions should more realistically overlap, and write down the generalisation in
which the sharp set is replaced by a weighting function $p(\mathrm{life}\,|\,F)$.
What this note adds is the magnitude of the quantity that generalisation would need.

An inexpensive intermediate step is available, and it needs nothing that is not
already published. Reading the quoted credible interval endpoints as the 16th and
84th percentiles and 11.2 as the median fixes a split normal, which returns 0.76 for
the posterior mass above the adopted ceiling against the 0.77 that \citet{wogan2026}
tabulate. The same reconstruction puts more than 0.98 of the posterior above the
maximum of \citet{guzman2013} and above any realised estimate, although below the
quoted interval that is an extrapolation and only the qualitative statement is
intended. Reporting the biosignature probability as a function of the assumed
ceiling, rather than at a single value, therefore costs a line of arithmetic on a
posterior that already exists. The corresponding Bayes factor cannot be recovered
this way, since it also requires the prior mass implied by the retrieval at each
candidate ceiling.

\section{Limitations} \label{sec:limits}

The estimates in Figure~\ref{fig:ladder} describe different reservoirs that are only
partly additive, and several of the seafloor values are alternative estimates of one
quantity rather than separate contributions to it, so the figure should be read as a
range of published values and not as a distribution. They also differ in what they
measure: the mid-ocean ridge values of \citet{keir2010} and \citet{cannat2010} are
fluxes released at vents, whereas \citet{merdith2020} model production from the iron
oxidation state of serpentinite and note that much of it is consumed in the
subsurface, which is why their values sit higher. A ceiling for a planet other than
Earth would also need its crustal production rate scaled, as \citet{guzman2013} do
for a five Earth mass planet.

\section{Software and data} \label{sec:data}
